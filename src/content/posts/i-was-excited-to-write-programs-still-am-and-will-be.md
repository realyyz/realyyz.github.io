> Programming matters not only because it can be a career, but because it lets an individual speak directly to general-purpose computation.

This essay is not trying to say that programmers will never be replaced. It is trying to say something simpler, and to me more important: programming was exciting because it gave individuals a direct interface to a general cognitive machine.

That excitement has not disappeared just because LLMs can now write code.

## The Information Revolution Was Different

Steam and electricity amplified human muscle, energy, transportation, lighting, and industrial production. They changed daily life enormously, but most people entered those systems as workers, consumers, passengers, or residents. They could use the systems, but they could rarely modify them.

The PC era was different. Microprocessors and personal computers placed a general-purpose cognitive machine on an ordinary person's desk. Writing, spreadsheets, layout, drawing, design, automation, games, and software all became things an individual could touch, modify, and control.

The deepest difference was not simply that computers were powerful. It was that they were programmable.

The same machine could become a notebook, a calculator, a database, a studio, a game console, or a communication tool just by changing software. This is why programmers had such unusual leverage for decades: they stood on top of a toolchain that was reproducible, composable, automatable, and almost free to copy.

## LLMs Lower the Gate, but They Are Not AGI

LLMs are weakening the old barrier between human intention and machine execution. Natural language is becoming a high-level interface to computation. A person can describe a goal, ask for code, inspect errors, request tests, refactor modules, connect APIs, and then judge whether the result makes sense.

This is a major change. It gives more people part of the leverage that programmers once held almost exclusively.

But that does not mean today's LLMs are AGI. Their ability still grows through external engineering systems: researchers, data pipelines, GPU clusters, evaluation suites, alignment work, deployment platforms, and capital. A frontier model does not yet reliably choose its own research agenda, train its successor, evaluate the result, deploy the next generation, and continue the loop as an autonomous subject.

It can help humans improve systems. It is not yet a stable self-improving intelligence.

## A More Democratic Interface, a More Centralized Foundation

There is an irony here. AI feels democratic at the interface layer: everyone can open a chat box and talk to a machine in ordinary language. But at the foundation layer, the most powerful models, chips, data centers, cloud platforms, and distribution channels are increasingly concentrated in the hands of a few companies and wealthy owners.

The PC era placed computation on the personal desk. The AI era may place the interface in everyone's browser while keeping the decisive infrastructure behind corporate gates.

That is both exciting and disappointing. Ordinary people are getting stronger tools, but they may also become more dependent on platforms they cannot inspect, replace, or leave easily.

## Why I Still Expect Strong AI

I do not have a religious view of the mind. I tend to understand human intelligence as something produced by the physical organ of the brain. If a material system can produce understanding, planning, creativity, self-monitoring, and judgment, then I see no principled reason why another material system could never exceed the brain.

Today's systems are not there yet. But that does not prove the boundary is sacred. It only proves that the technology has not arrived.

If strong AI appears, its importance will go far beyond job loss. Human beings have invented many powerful tools, but strong AI may be the first thing we invent that is better than us at inventing. After that, the most important historical turns may no longer be "humans invented X", but "AI invented X".

## Programming Will Still Matter

Even if strong AI arrives, and even if writing code loses much of its economic value, programming will still matter.

Humans can no longer beat the best Go AIs, but people still play Go. Humans cannot outrun cars, but people still run. Calculators surpassed human arithmetic, but mathematics did not lose its beauty.

Programming is not only a production tool. It is a way of thinking with systems. It forces you to define a problem, draw boundaries, handle exceptions, accept feedback, and revise structure. Whether a program works does not depend on your self-image. The system either accepts your expression or it does not.

That feedback is cold, but honest.

So the sentence "I was excited to write programs. Still am. And will be." is not a professional slogan. It is a posture toward the world. It says that the world is not only a finished product to be consumed. The world is also a system that can be understood, reorganized, and rewritten.
